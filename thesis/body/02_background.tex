\chapter{Background and Related Work}
\label{ch:background}

This chapter introduces the concepts the thesis builds on and positions its contribution against
published work. It is organised by the questions the thesis has to answer rather than by
technique: how a mobile robot navigates, how it decides what to do, how safety is enforced in a
learned policy, how energy enters the decision, and how such systems are validated. The chapter
closes by naming the gap that \cref{ch:models} addresses.

\section{Mobile robots and their navigation}
\label{sec:nav2}

Autonomous mobile robots (AMRs) move material between machines, stores and assembly stations,
localising and planning routes themselves rather than following fixed markers. Surveys cover the
navigation side thoroughly \cite{tang2025pathplanningreview, katona2024obstacleavoidance,
aremu2025pathplanningreview}: path planning and obstacle avoidance are mature topics with
established solutions. What remains open is the layer above --- the assignment of transport work,
a decision under uncertainty about when the next part will be finished and which machine will fail
next \cite{warita2024onlineplanning}. That is the problem this thesis addresses.

The robot here uses ROS~2 \cite{macenski2022ros2} with the Nav2 navigation stack
\cite{macenski2020nav2}, the de-facto standard in research and increasingly in industry. Four of
its components matter.

A \textbf{costmap} represents the hall as an occupancy grid inflated by the robot's footprint, so
that a path which keeps to free cells is one the robot can physically follow. A \textbf{global
planner} searches that grid for a route to the goal; the planners compared in
\cref{sec:planner-study} are NavFn (Dijkstra and A$^{*}$ variants), Theta$^{*}$, which allows
any-angle paths and therefore produces straighter routes with fewer turns, and the Smac family
(a 2D variant and a state-lattice variant that plans kinematically feasible paths). A
\textbf{controller} --- here MPPI, a sampling-based model-predictive controller --- follows the
planned path while reacting to what the sensors currently see. \textbf{AMCL} localises the robot in
the map using its lidar, and a \textbf{behaviour tree} sequences the whole process, including
recovery behaviours when a goal cannot be reached.

The separation matters for scope: Nav2 answers \emph{how do I get there} and the decision models of
\cref{ch:models} answer \emph{where should I go and why}, a division that reported ROS~2 and Nav2
deployments in logistics follow as well \cite{borse2025robotinonav}.

\section{Deciding what to do next}
\label{sec:decision-making}

\subsection{Dispatching rules}
Classical scheduling addresses the assignment problem with dispatching rules --- serve the nearest
station, the one waiting longest, the one with the fullest buffer. They are transparent, need no
training, and remain the baseline any learned method is expected to beat; comparisons against such
rules are still the standard way of demonstrating that a learned scheduler is worth its complexity
\cite{chang2025hierarchicalagv}. Their weakness is that a rule cannot trade one objective against
another in a situation its author did not anticipate, which is precisely what the reference policy
of \cref{sec:rule-policy} demonstrates when the factory is overloaded.

\subsection{Learned scheduling}
Deep reinforcement learning has been applied to exactly this class of problem: scheduling AGVs in
flexible job shops with energy and throughput in one objective
\cite{cheng2025energysavingfjsp}, hierarchical scheduling that adapts to shop-floor conditions
\cite{chang2025hierarchicalagv}, and joint scheduling of production equipment and transport
vehicles with action masks constraining the choices \cite{zhang2025dualresource}. The last is the
closest published counterpart to the PPO baseline of \cref{sec:ppo}: the same algorithm family,
the same use of masking, and a problem of the same shape --- production and transport decided
together.

Proximal Policy Optimization \cite{schulman2017ppo} is the algorithm used here, in its
mask-supporting variant, with the implementation from Stable-Baselines3 \cite{raffin2021sb3}.

\subsection{Neuro-symbolic policies}
Neuro-symbolic approaches combine learned components with explicit symbolic structure, with the
aim of keeping the behaviour inspectable. Recent work learns policies in a form that remains
readable and even editable after training \cite{baugh2025neuraldnfmt}; others derive transparency
from an explicit structured world model rather than from post-hoc explanation
\cite{hu2024graphworldmodels}, or argue that safety in human-robot settings must be symbolically
auditable rather than statistically inferred \cite{kozik2025explainable}.

The architecture of this thesis sits in that family but makes the opposite choice on one axis: its
symbolic layer is \emph{hand-written} rather than learned. The rules encode constraints that follow
from physics and from the problem statement (\cref{sec:constraints}), which are known exactly and
do not need to be induced from data; what is learned is only the part that genuinely requires
judgement. The cost of that choice --- a rule can be missing until reality exposes it --- is
discussed in \cref{sec:limitations}.

\section{Safety in learned policies}
\label{sec:safe-learning}

A learned policy optimises a reward; it does not, by construction, respect a constraint. Two
mechanisms are established for closing that gap.

\textbf{Shielding} places a correctness layer between the policy and the environment: the shield
filters out actions that would violate a specification, so the agent may learn freely within what
remains \cite{koenighofer2025shields}. The hard rules of \cref{sec:hard-rules} are a
domain-specific shield in exactly this sense, and the vocabulary of that literature --- a
correctness layer that is not itself learned --- describes them precisely.

\textbf{Action masking} is the practical form the same idea takes inside reinforcement learning
implementations: illegal actions are removed from the policy's distribution before sampling, as in
the AGV scheduling work cited above \cite{zhang2025dualresource}. The PPO baseline of this thesis
uses masking, which is what makes the comparison in \cref{ch:results} a comparison of
architectures rather than of information: both the neuro-symbolic model and PPO are told which
actions are legal.

Both mechanisms act on \emph{legality}. The contribution of this thesis acts on something else ---
the \emph{influence} a learned component may exert over choices that are all legal
(\cref{sec:bounded-correction}). The nearest published relative is shielding with adaptive
constraint strength \cite{odriozola2025fearfield}, where the severity of the constraint varies
rather than being binary; approaches that encode safety directly in the reward or in a risk-aware
objective \cite{long2025humanriskaware} take the opposite route, keeping safety inside the learned
function rather than outside it.

\section{Energy-aware decision making}
\label{sec:energy-aware}

Energy enters the problem twice: as a cost to be minimised and as a constraint that forces the
robot to interrupt work. Published scheduling approaches treat it in both ways. Battery management
is modelled as part of the scheduling decision, weighing the value of a transport task against the
need to charge under uncertainty about future demand \cite{dilefeld2025batterymanagement}; energy
and throughput are combined in a single objective for multi-AGV job shops
\cite{cheng2025energysavingfjsp, chang2025hierarchicalagv}; and charging itself is treated as a
learned decision rather than a fixed threshold \cite{zhou2025resilientcharging}, which is the
published justification for offering the robot a choice of charge levels rather than a single
\texttt{CHARGE} action (\cref{sec:actions}).

For the decision to be meaningful, the underlying energy model has to be credible. Reviews of
energy prediction for mobile robots \cite{gora2024energyprediction} place analytic models of the
kind used here --- idle power plus a distance term plus a payload term --- among the alternatives
and give consumption ranges against which such a model can be sanity-checked
(\cref{sec:models-shared}). Payload-dependent consumption has been estimated empirically for ground
robots \cite{mohamadi2024payloadpower}, which supports treating the payload term as proportional to
carried mass rather than as a free parameter. Measurements of which quantities actually dominate
consumption find angular velocity and acceleration to be the principal predictors, with payload
secondary \cite{rico2025explainableenergy} --- a result that independently explains why the
planner comparison of \cref{sec:planner-study} found large differences in turning but only small
ones in energy between planners that drive almost the same distance.

\section{Simulation and validation}
\label{sec:sim2real}

Learned decision policies are trained in simulation because the number of episodes required makes
anything else impractical, and the resulting question is always what the results are worth. The
two-layer pattern used in this thesis --- train and decide against a fast abstract model, then run
the result on the full system --- is the same pattern as digital-twin-driven approaches to AGV
optimisation \cite{lee2025digitaltwinagv}. Methodologies for quantifying the gap between a
simulated and a real navigation system, using multiple metrics rather than a single success rate,
have been proposed for flexible assembly systems \cite{bergs2025digitaltwinnavigation}; the
validation in \cref{sec:gazebo-validation} follows that spirit, comparing energy, throughput, lost
production and safety separately rather than reporting one aggregate number.

\section{Research gap}
\label{sec:gap}

The reviewed work divides along lines that leave one intersection empty. The scheduling literature
\cite{dilefeld2025batterymanagement, cheng2025energysavingfjsp, chang2025hierarchicalagv,
zhou2025resilientcharging, lee2025digitaltwinagv, warita2024onlineplanning} optimises energy and
throughput, but without an interpretable decision or a structural safety guarantee. The
neuro-symbolic literature \cite{baugh2025neuraldnfmt, hu2024graphworldmodels, kozik2025explainable}
provides interpretability, but not an industrial resource model in which battery, heat and wear
constrain what may be done. The safe-learning literature \cite{koenighofer2025shields,
odriozola2025fearfield, long2025humanriskaware} provides the safety machinery, but is usually
demonstrated without an energy objective or a full navigation stack. The energy literature
\cite{gora2024energyprediction, rico2025explainableenergy, mohamadi2024payloadpower} models
consumption accurately, but decides nothing.

This thesis occupies that intersection. It combines (i) a symbolic layer that makes safety
structural rather than learned, (ii) a \emph{bounded} neural correction on top of it, which
constrains the learned component's influence rather than only its choices, (iii) a calibrated
energy, thermal and wear model that makes the constraints physically meaningful, and (iv)
validation of the \emph{same policy object} in a fast simulator and in Gazebo with a complete Nav2
stack. Each ingredient exists separately in the literature; the combination, and the evidence in
\cref{ch:results} that the bound is what makes the learned part safe to keep, is what this work
contributes.
