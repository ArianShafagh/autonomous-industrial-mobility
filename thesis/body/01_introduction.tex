\chapter{Introduction}
\label{ch:introduction}

\section{Motivation}
\label{sec:motivation}

Picture a production hall late in a shift. Three machines are making parts at different speeds:
one finishes a part every two minutes, another every three, the third only every twelve, but its
parts are the heaviest. Each machine sets its output on a small buffer beside it, and when that
buffer is full the machine stops --- not because anything broke, but because there is nowhere left
to put the next part. A single mobile robot moves between them, collecting finished parts and
bringing them to one delivery point, returning to one charging station when its battery runs low.
Nobody tells it where to go.

The robot's difficulty is not driving. Modern navigation software plans routes through a cluttered
hall, follows them, and recovers when a corridor turns out to be blocked; that problem is solved
well enough to be bought off the shelf. The difficulty is deciding \emph{what to do next}. Serving
the fast machine first keeps the busiest line running, but it is the machine furthest from the
charging station. Collecting a half-empty buffer wastes a journey; waiting for it to fill risks
arriving after the line has already stopped, and every minute a line stands still is production
that is lost for good, not postponed. Charging early costs working time the shift will not give
back; charging late risks a robot stranded in a corridor, which requires a person to walk in and
fetch it --- exactly the outcome an autonomous system exists to avoid.

These decisions are made continuously, from a state that keeps changing and is partly random:
buffers fill irregularly, machines degrade and break down, the battery drains faster when the robot
is loaded or hot. This is the problem industrial mobile robots face once navigation works, and it
is the subject of this thesis.

\section{Problem statement}
\label{sec:problem-statement}

Two established ways of answering \emph{what next} exist, and each has a characteristic weakness.

A \textbf{rule-based} policy is written by hand: serve blocked lines first, charge when the battery
would not cover the trip home, do not drive half empty. Such a policy is predictable, explains
itself, and never does anything its author did not intend. What it cannot do is weigh alternatives
against each other. It has no way of judging whether this half-full buffer is worth a detour given
where the robot already is, how much energy the trip would cost, and which other line will fill
next.

A \textbf{learned} policy, trained by reinforcement learning, is good at exactly that weighing. It
discovers trade-offs nobody wrote down. But what it learns, it learns from a reward signal, and a
reward signal expresses a preference, not a guarantee. A policy can be trained to avoid draining
its battery and will still, in a situation its training did not cover, drive until the battery is
nearly empty --- because nothing in its construction forbids it.

The engineering question is therefore not which of the two is better, but whether they can be
combined so that the guarantee of the first survives the flexibility of the second. This thesis
builds such a combination --- a \emph{neuro-symbolic} policy, in which explicit rules decide what
is permitted and urgent, and a small neural network chooses among what remains --- and measures it
against a deep reinforcement learning policy given exactly the same information, in a complete
ROS~2 system with a simulated factory, a physically modelled robot and a real navigation stack.

\section{Research questions}
\label{sec:research-questions}

\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{RQ\arabic*.}, leftmargin=*, itemsep=0.25em]
  \item Does a neuro-symbolic policy outperform a deep reinforcement learning policy with the same
        information, measured on throughput, energy and safety?
  \item What does each half contribute --- do the rules alone, or the network alone, already
        suffice?
  \item Do the policies still behave sensibly in factories they were never trained for?
  \item Do results obtained in a fast, abstract simulator transfer to the full simulation with
        physics and a real navigation stack?
\end{enumerate}

RQ1 and RQ2 are answered in \cref{sec:overall,sec:ablation-results}, RQ3 in
\cref{sec:generalisation}, and RQ4 in \cref{sec:gazebo-validation}; \cref{sec:answers} draws the
four answers together.

\section{Contributions}
\label{sec:contributions}

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*, itemsep=0.25em]
  \item \textbf{A complete autonomous system}, not a simulation study: a maze-like factory
        generated from a single description, three production lines that lose output when their
        buffers fill, a robot with modelled energy, heat and wear, a mission executor with a
        navigation recovery ladder, a decision service the robot survives without, and a read-only
        live monitor (\cref{ch:system}).

  \item \textbf{A neuro-symbolic decision model} whose hard rules cannot be overruled, whose
        priorities are explicit, and which explains every decision in terms an engineer can check
        (\cref{ch:models}). Over \num{3240} measured shifts it is the strongest policy that never
        breaks a constraint: \num{53.81} against \num{52.35} for deep reinforcement learning and
        \num{41.66} for a rule-based reference (\cref{sec:overall}).

  \item \textbf{A bounded neural correction}, the central technical contribution: the network may
        adjust the analytic value of an action by at most one score unit relative to its
        alternatives, settling close calls but never overturning clear evidence
        (\cref{sec:bounded-correction}). It was motivated by an observation --- unbounded, the
        network carried a habit from its training scenarios into an overloaded factory it had never
        seen and waited while all three production lines stood still --- and, selected on training
        scenarios alone, is worth \num{3.87} points on scenarios the model had never seen.

  \item \textbf{An evaluation designed to be believed}: nine policies on identical seeds, six
        scenarios written after training and never used for it, paired non-parametric statistics,
        and validation of the fast simulator against complete shifts in Gazebo, where energy agrees
        to \SI{0.2}{\percent} and the safety difference reproduces
        (\cref{ch:setup,sec:gazebo-validation}), together with a planner study whose lesson
        transfers: the planner selected on short benchmark tours proved unreliable over full shifts
        (\cref{sec:planner-study}).
\end{itemize}

\section{Outline}
\label{sec:outline}

\Cref{ch:background} reviews the work this thesis builds on: navigation with ROS~2 and Nav2,
rule-based and learned decision making for mobile robots, safety in learned policies, energy-aware
scheduling, and how such systems are usually validated. \Cref{ch:problem} defines the factory, the
robot, the actions, the objective and the constraints exactly, and argues from the setting's own
arithmetic why no simple rule solves it. \Cref{ch:system} describes the system that executes and
measures decisions, and the design choices that make its numbers trustworthy.
\Cref{ch:models} develops the four decision models: the rule-based reference, the neuro-symbolic
model with its hard rules, priority tiers and bounded neural ranking, the reinforcement learning
comparison, and the ablations that isolate each part. \Cref{ch:setup} states how the comparison
was conducted, and \cref{ch:results} reports what happened. \Cref{ch:discussion} interprets those
results, names the measurement decisions that changed the conclusions, and sets out the limits of
the work. \Cref{ch:conclusion} concludes and identifies what should be done next.
