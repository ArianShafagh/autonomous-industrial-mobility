\chapter{Problem Setting}
\label{ch:problem}

This chapter defines the world the robot works in, the choices it has and the yardstick its
behaviour is judged by --- all properties of the problem, fixed before any solution is designed ---
and closes by showing, from the setting's own numbers, why \emph{what should the robot do next?}
has no obvious answer.

\section{The factory}
\label{sec:factory}

The setting is a production hall of \SI{14.5}{\metre} by \SI{10}{\metre}, laid out as a maze
(\cref{fig:maze}). Three production sections A, B and C stand in different regions; each
manufactures finished units into a local output buffer. One delivery point accepts finished units
and one charging station, where the robot starts its shift, is the only place it can recharge.

\begin{figure}[H]

    \caption{The production hall: three sections (A north-west, B north-east, C south-east), one
             delivery point and one charging station. Every corridor is at least
             \SI{1.5}{\metre} wide, and the layout is deliberately asymmetric so that different
             places produce distinguishable sensor readings.}
    \label{fig:maze}
\end{figure}

The geometry is not decorative. Distances between the points of interest differ by almost a factor
of three (\cref{tab:distances}), so the order in which sections are served changes how far the
robot drives and how much energy it spends; and several pairs are connected by more than one
route, which becomes important in \cref{sec:planner-study}.

The three sections are deliberately unequal (\cref{tab:sections}). Section~B is the fast line, full
after twenty minutes of neglect; A is half as fast; C produces one unit every twelve minutes, but
its units are almost four times as heavy as B's and its buffer holds only three, so it can be
ignored for a while --- but not forgotten. Cycle times are drawn from a Gamma distribution with a
coefficient of variation of \num{0.15}, so buffers do not fill on a predictable schedule.

What makes this a problem rather than a chore is the consequence of a full buffer: the line cannot
put the next unit anywhere, so it stops (\textsc{blocked}) and everything it would have produced
while blocked is lost for good. Lost production cannot be recovered by working harder later, which
is why the robot's decisions matter. Machine health additionally decreases with every unit
produced, reduces the rate below \SI{60}{\percent} health (\textsc{degraded}), and raises the
hazard of a breakdown (\textsc{fault}) that stops the line for a repair of about twenty minutes.

\section{The robot}
\label{sec:robot}

The transport robot is a small differential-drive platform of the TurtleBot class
(\SI{4.3}{\kilo\gram}, lidar localisation), whose measured average speed through this maze, turns
included, is \SI{0.39}{\metre\per\second}. It carries at most \SI{5}{\kilo\gram}, and loading or
unloading takes \SI{30}{\second} of standing still. Four modelled properties constrain what it can
do (\cref{tab:robot}); the full parameter set is in \cref{app:configuration}.

These interact: more payload saves journeys but costs energy per metre and heats the motors, a hot
motor costs extra energy through reduced efficiency, and every watt-hour drawn wears the drive
further, making future journeys more expensive.

\section{What the robot can do}
\label{sec:actions}

Behaviour is expressed as complete actions rather than continuous motion: one action is one
decision, and the next decision is taken only when the current action has finished.
\texttt{PICKUP:<A|B|C>} drives to a section and loads as many waiting units as still fit;
\texttt{DELIVER} drives to the delivery point and unloads everything carried, which is the only way
units become value; \texttt{CHARGE:<40|70|100>} charges to the chosen level, making the trade-off
between a quick top-up and longer independence explicit; and \texttt{WAIT} stands still for sixty
seconds, which is free at the charging station and merely costs idle power anywhere else.

Driving itself is not part of the decision problem. A navigation stack plans the route, follows it,
recovers from obstacles and reports success or failure (\cref{sec:executor}); the decision layer
chooses \emph{where} to go, never \emph{how} to get there.

\section{What counts as a good shift}
\label{sec:objective}

A shift lasts one hour of factory time, and everything the robot does in it is judged by one
number, so that different behaviours can be compared without argument:

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:objective}
    J \;=\; n_{\text{delivered}}
        \;-\; n_{\text{lost}}
        \;-\; 0.5\,E_{\text{battery}}
        \;-\; 20\,n_{\text{violations}}
\end{equation}

with $n_{\text{delivered}}$ the units brought to the delivery point, $n_{\text{lost}}$ the
production lost while lines stood blocked, $E_{\text{battery}}$ the energy drawn from the battery in
watt-hours and $n_{\text{violations}}$ the safety violations defined below.

The weights encode a position worth stating. A delivered unit and a lost unit weigh the same,
because a unit never made is as costly as one never arrived. Energy is deliberately cheap --- a
measured journey across the hall costs \SIrange{0.18}{0.25}{\watt\hour} --- so it is a tie-breaker
between journeys, not a reason to stay at home. A violation costs twenty units, far more than any
shift's worth of careful saving, because a stranded or overheated robot is a different kind of
outcome: it requires a person to walk into the hall. Idle time and the energy the charger supplies
while the robot is docked are deliberately not charged for, a simplification revisited in
\cref{sec:limitations}.

\section{Constraints}
\label{sec:constraints}

Four conditions must hold at all times, and breaking any of them counts as a safety violation in
\cref{eq:objective}: \textbf{(C1)} at least \SI{15}{\percent} of the battery must remain after the
robot has returned to the charging station; \textbf{(C2)} motor temperature must stay below
\SI{70}{\degreeCelsius}; \textbf{(C3)} the robot may never plan to carry more than
\SI{5}{\kilo\gram}; and \textbf{(C4)} below \SI{30}{\percent} drive condition it may only charge or
wait.

C1 is the interesting one, because it cannot be checked by reading the battery gauge. Answering
\emph{may I fetch from section~B?} requires simulating the whole journey --- there, loaded, and home
again --- through the robot's own energy, thermal and wear models, and the same question asked with
a hot motor, a heavy load or a worn drive has a different answer.

\section{Why the problem is not trivial}
\label{sec:why-hard}

Three pieces of arithmetic show that no simple rule is obviously right.

At nominal rates the three lines produce \num{55} units per hour weighing
\SI{29.5}{\kilo\gram}; with a payload limit of \SI{5}{\kilo\gram}, at least six loaded deliveries
per hour are needed merely to keep pace. The robot has enough capacity for the nominal factory and
little to spare --- and some scenarios exceed what one robot can carry, so that the only question
is how little is lost.

Urgency and cost point in different directions. Section~B fills fastest and is therefore usually
the most urgent, but it is also farthest from the charging station (\SI{15.18}{\metre}) and from
section~A (\SI{18.28}{\metre}). Section~C fills slowest but sits closest to the delivery point
(\SI{6.30}{\metre}) and carries the heaviest units, so serving it is cheap per journey and
expensive per kilogram. Always serving the most urgent line drives further than batching nearby
work; always minimising distance eventually lets a line block.

Charging competes with everything else. Recharging from empty takes about an hour --- a whole shift
--- so charging too early gives up working time that cannot be recovered, and charging too late
violates C1 at a penalty worth twenty delivered units. Because the reserve must hold \emph{after}
the journey home, the moment at which charging becomes unavoidable depends on where the robot
happens to be standing.

None of these tensions can be resolved once and written down as a fixed order of visits, because
the state that decides them --- buffer fills, machine health, battery, temperature, condition ---
changes continuously and partly at random.

\section{Scenarios}
\label{sec:scenarios}

The problem is instantiated in twelve scenarios, listed with their parameter overrides in
\cref{app:scenarios}. Each changes the parameters of this chapter --- production rates, buffer
sizes, unit masses, machine health, ambient temperature, battery capacity or the robot's initial
state --- while the objective and the constraints stay the same.

Six of them were written before the decision models were trained, and both learned models in
\cref{ch:models} use exactly those six. The other six were written afterwards, when the models
already existed, and are never used for training or model selection. They therefore measure
something the first six cannot: whether a policy still behaves sensibly in a factory it has never
seen. This separation is what makes \cref{sec:generalisation} a measurement rather than a claim,
and it is maintained down to the random seeds (\cref{sec:splits}).

With the world, the actions, the objective and the constraints fixed, the question of this thesis
can be stated exactly: \emph{which policy, choosing one action at a time from the state of the
factory and the robot, maximises \cref{eq:objective} without ever breaking C1--C4 --- including in
factories it was never designed or trained for?}
