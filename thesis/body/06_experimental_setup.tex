\chapter{Experimental Setup}
\label{ch:setup}

This chapter states how the policies of \cref{ch:models} were measured. Three questions decide
whether such a comparison means anything: whether the policies were given the same problems,
whether they were tested on situations they had not been tuned for, and whether the reported
differences are larger than the noise.

\section{Evaluation protocol}
\label{sec:protocol}

The main experiment runs every policy on every scenario for thirty different random seeds:
\num{9} policies $\times$ \num{12} scenarios $\times$ \num{30} seeds $=$ \num{3240} shifts, each
one simulated hour of factory time.

A seed fixes everything random in a shift --- cycle times, when machines fail, how long repairs
take --- so the same seed produces the same factory for every policy and the comparison is
\emph{paired}: thirty matched pairs per scenario that differ only in the decisions taken. This
matters because variation between seeds is far larger than variation between policies, and pairing
removes it from the comparison instead of leaving it as noise. The fast simulator makes this
affordable: the full experiment takes \SI{78}{\second} on ten workers, which is what allows thirty
seeds rather than three and nine policies rather than two.

\section{Keeping training and testing apart}
\label{sec:splits}

A comparison of learned policies is only meaningful if they are measured on something they were
not tuned for. Two separations are enforced.

\paragraph{Scenarios.}
Six scenarios --- \texttt{balanced}, \texttt{high\_demand}, \texttt{one\_hot\_section},
\texttt{fault\_burst}, \texttt{low\_battery\_start} and \texttt{worn\_robot} --- are declared as
the training set, and both learned models use exactly those six: the neural scorer collects its
look-ahead data from them, and PPO trains on them. The other six (\cref{app:scenarios}) were
written after training, when both models already existed, and are used for evaluation only. They
are referred to as \emph{unseen} throughout, and results on them are reported separately
(\cref{sec:generalisation}) rather than averaged away.

\paragraph{Seeds.}
Test shifts use seeds \numrange{5000}{5029}, which no training or model selection touches:
look-ahead collection uses seeds from \num{12345} upwards, the best training round is selected on
\numrange{1000}{1003}, the bound $\beta$ on \numrange{1000}{1019}, and PPO draws random 31-bit
seeds. Both separations are declared in \file{params.yaml} and read by the training and evaluation
tools alike, so an evaluation cannot silently drift into its training data.

\section{Metrics}
\label{sec:metrics}

The primary metric is the shift score of \cref{eq:objective}, reported together with the quantities
it is composed of, because two policies can reach the same score for opposite reasons: delivered
units (throughput); lost production (the cost of a decision not taken); energy, in watt-hours drawn
from the battery and per delivered unit; charging trips and minimum battery, which show how a policy
manages its energy budget; unsafe shifts; and decision latency, excluding the first decision of a
shift, which loads the model.

A \emph{safety violation} is recorded when the battery falls below the reserve away from the
charger, when the motor temperature reaches its limit, or when the battery is empty. Repeated
occurrences of the same kind count once, so the count measures how often a policy entered an unsafe
state, not how long it stayed there. The score charges \num{20} points per violation, which is
still not enough to make an unsafe policy's score comparable to a safe one's, so the two numbers are
always reported together.

\section{Statistics}
\label{sec:statistics}

Means are reported with \SI{95}{\percent} bootstrap confidence intervals (\num{2000} resamples),
because shift scores are not normally distributed: in scenarios with random failures the
distribution is skewed by shifts in which an early fault cost most of the hour. Differences are
tested with the \emph{paired Wilcoxon signed-rank test} on the matched seeds at a significance level
of \num{0.05} --- paired for the reason given above, non-parametric because it assumes nothing about
the distribution's shape. The \NS is the reference throughout, so a $p$-value always answers the
same question: \emph{is this policy's score different from the neuro-symbolic model's, on the same
shifts?}

Two caveats. No correction for multiple comparisons is applied, so with eight policies across
twelve scenarios some individually significant results are expected by chance, which is why the
discussion relies on differences that are consistent across scenarios. And significance is not
importance: with thirty paired seeds half a point can reach $p<0.001$ while meaning little in a
factory, so effect sizes are always given beside the $p$-value.

\section{Validation in the full simulation}
\label{sec:gazebo-setup}

The fast simulator abstracts driving into distances, durations and energies; whether its numbers
describe the real system is answered by running complete shifts through the full stack --- Gazebo
physics, Nav2, AMCL, the real executor and the decision service --- and comparing each against the
fast simulator on the same policy, scenario and seed. The procedure for one validation shift is:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*, itemsep=0.15em]
  \item Start the complete system headless with the chosen scenario, model, seed and shift length.
  \item Wait for the mission summary the executor writes when the shift ends, then stop that run
        and confirm that no process from it survives --- a leftover simulator would publish a
        second clock and corrupt the next run.
  \item Compute the score from the run's own logs, applying the definitions of
        \cref{sec:metrics}: delivered units from the mission summary, lost production from the
        per-section logs, energy from the robot telemetry, and violations from the recorded
        battery and temperature traces.
  \item Replay the same policy, scenario, seed and shift length in the fast simulator and compute
        the same quantities.
\end{enumerate}

One detail decides whether the comparison is fair. The executor checks the clock only between
actions, so an action that starts before the shift ends is always finished --- in one case a
seventeen-minute charge that began in the last minute. The fast simulator normally stops exactly at
the shift length, mid-action, so for the comparison it is run in a mode that also finishes the
running action; without this it would appear to use systematically less energy purely because it
stopped earlier.

Twelve validation shifts of twenty minutes were driven (two models $\times$ three scenarios
$\times$ two seeds) and two of sixty minutes on \texttt{low\_battery\_start}, where twenty minutes
is too short for the battery to bind. Wall-clock cost is why these are counted in tens rather than
thousands: about \SI{21}{\minute} of real time per twenty-minute shift.

\section{The planner study}
\label{sec:planner-setup}

The planner comparison isolates the global planner: every planner computes the path and the same
controller follows it. It was run at two levels --- a fixed seven-leg tour repeated five times per
planner with the order rotated, measuring planning time, path length, turning and clearance; then
complete twenty-minute shifts of the identical scenario and seed, five repeats per planner,
alternating planners so that background load affects both equally. That alternation is what allows
the faults in \cref{sec:planner-study} to be attributed to the planner rather than the computer.

\section{Software and hardware}
\label{sec:hardware}

All experiments ran on one laptop --- Intel Core i7-9750H (6 cores, \SI{2.6}{\giga\hertz}),
\SI{23}{\gibi\byte} RAM, no GPU --- with ROS~2 Jazzy, Nav2 (AMCL, MPPI) and Gazebo Harmonic
\num{8.11} headless, Python \num{3.12}, PyTorch \num{2.14} (CPU) and Stable-Baselines3 \num{2.9}
with \texttt{sb3-contrib}. That training and inference run on a CPU is itself worth noting: these
models are small enough that a factory would need no special hardware.

Training the neural scorer takes about \SI{40}{\minute} for three rounds, of which roughly
\SI{33}{\minute} is look-ahead simulation; PPO takes \SI{9.8}{\minute} for \num{300000} steps. The
full evaluation of \num{3240} shifts takes \SI{78}{\second}, against about \SI{21}{\minute} per
Gazebo shift --- the practical reason the experiment has two tiers at all
(\cref{sec:two-simulators}).
