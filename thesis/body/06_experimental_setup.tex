\chapter{Experimental Setup}
\label{ch:setup}

This chapter states how the policies of \cref{ch:models} were measured, in enough detail that a
reader who doubts a number in \cref{ch:results} can find out exactly how it was produced. Three
questions decide whether such a comparison means anything: whether the policies were given the
same problems, whether they were tested on situations they had not been tuned for, and whether the
differences that are reported are larger than the noise. Each is answered below, followed by the
protocol for the full-simulation validation and the software and hardware used.

\section{Evaluation protocol}
\label{sec:protocol}

The main experiment runs every policy on every scenario for thirty different random seeds:
\num{9} policies $\times$ \num{12} scenarios $\times$ \num{30} seeds $=$ \num{3240} shifts, each
one simulated hour of factory time.

A seed fixes everything random in a shift: the production cycle times of the three sections, the
moments at which machines fail and how long repairs take. The same seed therefore produces
exactly the same factory for every policy, so the comparison is \emph{paired}: for any two
policies there are thirty matched pairs of shifts per scenario that differ only in the decisions
taken. This matters because the variation between seeds is far larger than the variation between
policies --- scores within a single scenario routinely differ by more between two seeds than the
policies differ from each other on average --- and pairing removes that variation from the
comparison instead of leaving it as noise.

The fast simulator makes this affordable: the full experiment takes \SI{78}{\second} on ten
worker processes, which is what allows thirty seeds rather than three, and nine policies rather
than two.

\section{Keeping training and testing apart}
\label{sec:splits}

A comparison of learned policies is only meaningful if they are measured on something they were
not tuned for. Two separations are enforced.

\paragraph{Scenarios.}
Six scenarios --- \texttt{balanced}, \texttt{high\_demand}, \texttt{one\_hot\_section},
\texttt{fault\_burst}, \texttt{low\_battery\_start} and \texttt{worn\_robot} --- are declared as
the training set, and both learned models use exactly those six: the neural scorer collects its
look-ahead data from them, and PPO trains on them. The other six (\cref{tab:scenarios}) were
written after training, when both models already existed, and are used for evaluation only. They
are referred to as \emph{unseen} throughout, and results on them are reported separately
(\cref{sec:generalisation}) rather than averaged away.

\paragraph{Seeds.}
Test shifts use seeds \numrange{5000}{5029}. No seed in that range is used anywhere in training or
model selection: the look-ahead collection uses seeds from \num{12345} upwards, the selection of
the best training round uses \numrange{1000}{1003}, the bound $\beta$ was selected on
\numrange{1000}{1019}, and PPO draws random 31-bit seeds during training. The development seeds
overlap each other, which is harmless --- they are all part of building the model --- but none of
them touches the test range.

Both separations are declared in the configuration file rather than in a script, so an evaluation
cannot silently drift into its training data; the test seed range and the list of training
scenarios are read from \file{params.yaml} by the training and evaluation tools alike.

\section{Metrics}
\label{sec:metrics}

The primary metric is the objective of \cref{eq:objective}, the shift score. It is reported
together with the quantities it is composed of, because two policies can reach the same score for
opposite reasons:

\begin{description}[leftmargin=1.2em, style=nextline, itemsep=0.15em]
  \item[Delivered units] --- what the factory gets: the throughput.
  \item[Lost production] --- units not made because a buffer was full and its line stood
        \textsc{blocked}. This is the cost of a decision not taken.
  \item[Energy] --- watt-hours drawn from the battery over the shift, and watt-hours per delivered
        unit, which is the efficiency figure comparable across policies of different throughput.
  \item[Charging trips] and \textbf{minimum battery} --- how a policy manages its energy budget:
        many short charges and a high floor, or few charges and a low one.
  \item[Unsafe shifts] --- the number of shifts containing at least one safety violation.
  \item[Decision latency] --- milliseconds per decision, excluding the first decision of a shift,
        which loads the model.
\end{description}

A \emph{safety violation} is recorded when the battery falls below the reserve while the robot is
away from the charger, when the motor temperature reaches its limit, or when the battery is empty.
Repeated occurrences of the same kind in a row count once, so the count measures how often a policy
entered an unsafe state, not how long it stayed there. The score charges \num{20} points per
violation, which --- as \cref{sec:overall} notes --- is still not enough to make an unsafe policy's
score comparable to a safe one's; the two numbers are therefore always reported together.

\section{Statistics}
\label{sec:statistics}

Means are reported with \SI{95}{\percent} bootstrap confidence intervals (\num{2000} resamples of
the shifts of that policy and scenario). The bootstrap is used because shift scores are not
normally distributed: in scenarios with random machine failures the distribution is skewed by
shifts in which an early fault cost most of the hour.

Differences between policies are tested with the \emph{paired Wilcoxon signed-rank test} on the
matched seeds, at a significance level of \num{0.05}. The test is paired for the reason given in
\cref{sec:protocol}, and non-parametric because it makes no assumption about the shape of the
score distribution. The \NS is the reference in every comparison, so a reported $p$-value always
answers the same question: \emph{is this policy's score different from the neuro-symbolic model's,
on the same shifts?}

Two honest caveats. First, no correction for multiple comparisons is applied: with eight policies
compared against the reference across twelve scenarios, some individually significant results are
expected by chance alone, which is why the discussion relies on differences that are both
significant and consistent across scenarios rather than on isolated ones. Second, statistical
significance is not importance: with thirty paired seeds, a difference of half a point can reach
$p<0.001$ while meaning little in a factory. Effect sizes are therefore always given in the same
sentence as the $p$-value.

\section{Validation in the full simulation}
\label{sec:gazebo-setup}

Every number in \cref{sec:overall,sec:generalisation,sec:safety-results} comes from the fast
simulator, which abstracts driving into distances, durations and energies. Whether those numbers
describe the real system is a separate question, and it is answered by running complete shifts
through the full stack --- Gazebo physics, Nav2 planning and control, AMCL localisation, the real
mission executor and the decision service --- and comparing each of them against the fast
simulator on the same policy, scenario and seed.

The procedure for one validation shift is:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*, itemsep=0.15em]
  \item Start the complete system headless with the chosen scenario, model, seed and shift length.
  \item Wait for the mission summary the executor writes when the shift ends, then stop that run
        and confirm that no process from it survives --- a leftover simulator would publish a
        second clock and corrupt the next run.
  \item Compute the score from the run's own logs, applying the definitions of
        \cref{sec:metrics}: delivered units from the mission summary, lost production from the
        per-section logs, energy from the robot telemetry, and violations from the recorded
        battery and temperature traces.
  \item Replay the same policy, scenario, seed and shift length in the fast simulator and compute
        the same quantities.
\end{enumerate}

One detail decides whether the comparison is fair. The mission executor checks the clock only
between actions, so an action that starts before the shift ends is always finished --- a shift can
therefore overrun, and in one case an action that began in the last minute was a seventeen-minute
charge. The fast simulator normally stops at exactly the shift length, mid-action. For the
comparison it is therefore run in a mode that also finishes the running action, so that both sides
account for the same work. Without this, the fast simulator would appear to use systematically less
energy, purely because it stopped earlier.

Twelve validation shifts of twenty minutes were driven (two models $\times$ three scenarios
$\times$ two seeds), and two of sixty minutes on \texttt{low\_battery\_start}, because twenty
minutes is too short for the battery to become the binding constraint. Wall-clock cost is the
reason these are counted in tens and not thousands: a twenty-minute shift takes about
\SI{21}{\minute} of real time, and a sixty-minute shift a little over an hour.

\section{The planner study}
\label{sec:planner-setup}

The planner comparison of \cref{sec:planner-study} isolates the global planner: every planner
computes the path, and the same local controller follows it, so only the route-finding differs.
It was run at two levels. First on a fixed seven-leg tour of the hall, repeated five times per
planner with the order of planners rotated between repeats, measuring planning time, path length
against the shortest known path, turning and clearance. Then over complete twenty-minute shifts of
the identical scenario and seed, five repeats per planner, alternating planners so that background
load on the machine affects both equally --- this alternation is what allows the faults reported in
\cref{sec:planner-study} to be attributed to the planner rather than to the computer.

\section{Software and hardware}
\label{sec:hardware}

All experiments ran on one laptop: Intel Core i7-9750H (6 cores, 12 threads, \SI{2.6}{\giga\hertz}),
\SI{23}{\gibi\byte} RAM. No GPU was used: training and inference run on the CPU throughout,
which is itself a result worth noting: the models in this thesis are small enough that a factory
would not need special hardware to run them.

The software stack is ROS~2 Jazzy with Nav2 (AMCL localisation, MPPI controller) and Gazebo
Harmonic \num{8.11} running headless, Python \num{3.12}, PyTorch \num{2.14} (CPU build) for the
neural scorer, and Stable-Baselines3 \num{2.9} with \texttt{sb3-contrib} for Maskable PPO.

Training costs, on that machine: the neural scorer takes about \SI{40}{\minute} for three rounds
of collection and training, of which roughly \SI{33}{\minute} is look-ahead simulation; PPO takes
\SI{9.8}{\minute} for \num{300000} steps, and the unmasked variant the same. The full evaluation of
\num{3240} shifts takes \SI{78}{\second}. The Gazebo validation, by contrast, takes about
\SI{21}{\minute} per twenty-minute shift, which is the practical reason the experiment is
structured in two tiers at all (\cref{sec:two-simulators}).
