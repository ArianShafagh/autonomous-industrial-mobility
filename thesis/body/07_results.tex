\chapter{Results}
\label{ch:results}

This chapter reports what the policies of \cref{ch:models} actually did, under the protocol of
\cref{ch:setup}. It is deliberately descriptive: the numbers are stated, the differences that are
statistically supported are marked as such, and the interpretation is left to
\cref{ch:discussion}.

Unless stated otherwise, every figure in this chapter comes from the same experiment: nine
policies, twelve scenarios, thirty seeds each, one simulated hour per shift --- \num{3240} shifts
in total, all policies on identical seeds. Confidence intervals are \SI{95}{\percent} bootstrap
intervals of the mean score; $p$-values come from paired Wilcoxon signed-rank tests against the
\NS on the same seeds. The complete tables, including every metric for every scenario, are in the
result files listed in \cref{app:reproduction}.

One reading instruction matters throughout. Because a safety violation costs a fixed
\num{20} points per \emph{kind} of violation (\cref{sec:metrics}) rather than per minute spent in
the unsafe state, a policy that works through a violation can still finish a shift with a high
score. Such a score is not a valid result: it is obtained by ignoring a constraint that the
problem statement declares inviolable. Wherever a policy's number includes shifts with violations,
this is marked, and in \cref{fig:score-by-scenario} those bars are hatched.

\section{What each half contributes}
\label{sec:ablation-results}

The ablations isolate the two halves of the model. Switching the network off but keeping the rules
and the analytic estimate (\texttt{ns\_estimate\_only}) costs \num{0.96} points overall
($p<0.001$); replacing the analytic estimate by the symbolic layer's own cheapest-per-unit
ranking (\texttt{ns\_symbolic\_only}) costs \num{0.90} points ($p<0.001$). Removing the rules
instead and letting the network decide alone (\texttt{ns\_neural\_only}) produces a score that is
not significantly different from the full model over all scenarios ($p=0.109$) --- but reaches it
while violating a constraint in \num{57} of \num{360} shifts.

The most informative ablation is \texttt{ns\_unbounded}: the same network, the same rules, the
same training, with the only difference that its correction is not limited
(\cref{sec:bounded-correction}). Over all scenarios it scores \num{2.25} points less than the
bounded model ($p<0.001$). \Cref{tab:splits} shows where that difference comes from.

On the six scenarios the network was trained on, bounding its influence changes almost nothing
(\num{60.42} against \num{59.79}, $p=0.058$). On the six it never saw, the same bound is worth
\num{3.87} points ($p<0.001$). The bound therefore does not make the network better; it prevents
it from becoming harmful where its training no longer applies.

\section{Generalisation}
\label{sec:generalisation}

Every policy scores lower on the unseen scenarios than on the seen ones, which is expected: those
six scenarios were written to be harder. What differs is how much each policy loses and what it
sacrifices.

The clearest case is \texttt{heavy\_load}, a factory producing beyond what one robot can carry.
Here the unbounded model scores \num{33.78} and the bounded one \num{54.88}, a difference of
\num{21.11} points ($p<0.001$) --- by far the largest effect of the bound anywhere in the
evaluation, and the observation that motivated it (\cref{sec:bounded-correction}). Both variants
are safe; the difference is entirely lost production, \num{30.06} against \num{21.84} units.

Two policies score higher than the \NS under \texttt{heavy\_load}: \texttt{ns\_neural\_only} with
\num{77.73} and \texttt{ppo} with \num{59.47}. Both do so by working the battery down to a
minimum of \SI{3.4}{\percent} and \SI{1.4}{\percent} respectively, and both violate the reserve
constraint in \num{30} and \num{29} of their \num{30} shifts. Under the rules of
\cref{sec:constraints} these are not better solutions to the problem but solutions to a different,
unconstrained one.

\section{Safety}
\label{sec:safety-results}

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.82\textwidth]{fig_violations.png}
    \caption{Share of shifts containing at least one safety violation (battery below the reserve
             away from the charger, motor temperature at the limit, or an empty battery).}
    \label{fig:violations}
\end{figure}

The result is categorical rather than gradual. Every policy that carries the symbolic hard rules
--- the \NS, its bounded and unbounded variants, the estimate-only and symbolic-only ablations and
the rule-based reference --- completed all \num{1800} of their shifts without a single violation.
Every policy without them violated constraints: the same network without rules in
\SI{15.8}{\percent} of shifts, masked \texttt{ppo} in \SI{14.7}{\percent}, unmasked \texttt{ppo} in
\SI{11.7}{\percent}.

For \texttt{ppo} the violations are concentrated where its training no longer covers the
situation: \num{49} of its \num{53} unsafe shifts occur in unseen scenarios, and \num{29} of those
in \texttt{heavy\_load} alone. Retraining PPO on all six training scenarios instead of four did not
change this (\texttt{ppo\_wp6}, \num{51/360}), which indicates that the violations follow from
learning the constraint from a reward signal rather than from having seen too few scenarios.

\section{Throughput and energy}
\label{sec:energy-results}

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.78\textwidth]{fig_energy_throughput.png}
    \caption{Delivered units against energy per delivered unit, averaged over all scenarios and
             seeds. Points towards the lower right deliver more for less energy.}
    \label{fig:energy-throughput}
\end{figure}

The rule-based reference is by far the most energy-efficient policy (\SI{0.099}{\watt\hour} per
delivered unit against \SI{0.150}{\watt\hour} for the \NS), and it achieves this by charging often
--- \num{7.1} charging trips per shift against \num{2.8} --- and therefore spending much of the
shift parked, where idling is paid by the charger rather than by the battery. Its throughput
suffers accordingly: \num{51.6} delivered units against \num{59.9}, and \num{7.47} lost units
against \num{1.90}. This accounting asymmetry is a known limitation of the objective and is
discussed in \cref{sec:limitations}.

\texttt{ppo} sits at the opposite end: it delivers the most units of any policy
(\num{61.9}) and spends the most energy (\SI{10.21}{\watt\hour}, \SI{0.180}{\watt\hour} per unit),
with the lowest mean minimum battery of the safe-scoring policies (\SI{45.0}{\percent} against
\SI{59.0}{\percent} for the \NS). Its behaviour is consistent: drive more, charge less, stay closer
to the limits.

\section{Decision latency}
\label{sec:latency}

Mean time per decision was \SI{1.46}{\milli\second} for the \NS, \SI{1.39}{\milli\second} for the
symbolic layer alone, \SI{2.67}{\milli\second} for \texttt{ppo}, measured on the evaluation
machine excluding the first decision of each shift, which loads the model. Since one action lasts
between \SI{30}{\second} and several minutes, decision time is irrelevant to the result for every
policy tested; it is reported only to establish that neither approach is disqualified by its
computational cost.

\section{Validation against the full simulation}
\label{sec:gazebo-validation}

All numbers so far come from the fast simulator. To establish what they are worth, twelve complete
shifts were driven in Gazebo with the full navigation stack, and each was replayed in the fast
simulator with the same policy, scenario and seed (\cref{sec:gazebo-setup}).

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.6\textwidth]{fig_gazebo_vs_sim.png}
    \caption{Gazebo score against fast-simulator score for the same policy, scenario and seed. A
             point on the dashed line would mean perfect agreement.}
    \label{fig:gazebo-vs-sim}
\end{figure}

Over the eleven shifts that ran to completion, the fast simulator and Gazebo agree closely on
energy and slightly less on throughput: mean energy \SI{3.24}{\watt\hour} against
\SI{3.24}{\watt\hour} (\SI{+0.2}{\percent}), delivered units \num{23.7} against \num{25.0}
(\SI{-5.1}{\percent}), score \num{21.99} against \num{23.32} (\SI{-5.7}{\percent}). The Gazebo runs
are consistently the slightly weaker ones: real driving loses a few seconds per leg to
acceleration, recovery behaviours and arrival tolerances, which over a twenty-minute shift costs
about one delivered unit. No completed shift contained a safety violation on either side, and the
ordering of the policies was the same in both.

Twenty-minute shifts are, however, too short for the battery to become critical, which is where
the policies differ most. One full sixty-minute shift was therefore driven per model on
\texttt{low\_battery\_start} (\cref{tab:longshift}).

With almost identical throughput (\num{61} against \num{62} units), \texttt{ppo} drove the battery

below \SI{17.0}{\percent}. The safety difference measured statistically in
\cref{sec:safety-results} therefore reproduces in the full simulation, with real navigation,
real timing and real recovery behaviour.

\section{Global path planner}
\label{sec:planner-study}

The decision layer chooses destinations; a global planner chooses the route. Five Nav2 planners
were compared, first on short tours and then over complete shifts, with the same controller in
every case so that only the planner differs.

On the short benchmark --- a fixed seven-leg tour, five repeats per planner --- Theta$^{*}$ was the
fastest, completing a tour in \SI{147.8(9)}{\second} against \SI{152.7(19)}{\second} for the NavFn
Dijkstra planner ($p=0.001$), with about half the total turning and the shortest planning times.
On that evidence it was made the default.

Over full twenty-minute shifts the picture reversed. In five repetitions of an identical shift per
planner, three of the five Theta$^{*}$ shifts contained navigation faults --- six planner-loop
stalls, three refusals to plan and six navigation retries --- while all five NavFn shifts were
clean. The refusals carry their own explanation in the planner's message: \texttt{Either of the
start or goal pose are an obstacle!}. Theta$^{*}$ refuses to plan when the start or goal cell is
momentarily occupied in the costmap, which happens when the robot stands close to a machine;
NavFn plans from the nearest free cell within its tolerance of \SI{0.5}{\metre} instead. Counting
every Gazebo shift driven in this work, Theta$^{*}$ produced faults in \num{6} of \num{17} shifts
and NavFn in \num{0} of \num{5}. The speed advantage did not survive either: over whole shifts the
two planners scored \num{18.07} (sd \num{1.61}) and \num{17.75} (sd \num{1.23}), a difference well
inside the run-to-run spread. NavFn Dijkstra was therefore restored as the default.

\section{Summary}
\label{sec:results-summary}

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
  \item Among policies that never violated a constraint, the \NS scored highest overall
        (\num{53.81}), ahead of its symbolic layer alone (\num{52.91}, $p<0.001$) and the
        rule-based reference (\num{41.66}, $p<0.001$).
  \item Bounding the neural correction is worth almost nothing on the training scenarios
        ($p=0.058$) and \num{3.87} points on unseen ones ($p<0.001$), with the largest single
        effect in \texttt{heavy\_load} (\num{54.88} against \num{33.78}).
  \item Every policy carrying the symbolic rules completed \num{1800} shifts without a violation;
        every policy without them violated constraints in \SIrange{11.7}{15.8}{\percent} of shifts,
        mostly in scenarios outside their training.
  \item \texttt{ppo} matches the \NS on score in seen scenarios ($p=0.213$) by delivering more and
        spending \SI{21}{\percent} more energy, and is unsafe in \num{49} of its \num{180} unseen
        shifts.
  \item The fast simulator agrees with the full Gazebo simulation to \SI{0.2}{\percent} on energy
        and \SI{5.7}{\percent} on score, and the safety difference between the \NS and
        \texttt{ppo} reproduces in a full-length Gazebo shift.
  \item A planner selected on short tours proved unreliable over full shifts, and was replaced.
\end{itemize}
