\chapter{Results}
\label{ch:results}

This chapter reports what the policies of \cref{ch:models} actually did, under the protocol of
\cref{ch:setup}. It is deliberately descriptive: the numbers are stated, the differences that are
statistically supported are marked as such, and the interpretation is left to
\cref{ch:discussion}.

Unless stated otherwise, every number comes from the same experiment (\cref{sec:protocol}):
\num{3240} shifts, all policies on identical seeds, \SI{95}{\percent} bootstrap confidence
intervals, and paired Wilcoxon tests against the \NS.

One reading instruction matters throughout. A safety violation costs a fixed \num{20} points per
\emph{kind} of violation rather than per minute spent unsafe, so a policy that works through a
violation can still finish with a high score. Such a score is not a valid result: it is obtained by
ignoring a constraint the problem declares inviolable. Wherever a number includes shifts with
violations this is marked, and in \cref{fig:score-by-scenario} those bars are hatched.

\section{What each half contributes}
\label{sec:ablation-results}

Removing the rules and letting the network decide alone (\texttt{ns\_neural\_only}) gives a score
not significantly different from the full model ($p=0.109$) --- reached while violating a constraint
in \num{57} of \num{360} shifts. The most informative ablation is \texttt{ns\_unbounded}: the same
network, rules and training, with the only difference that its correction is not limited
(\cref{sec:bounded-correction}). It scores \num{2.25} points less overall ($p<0.001$), and
\cref{tab:splits} shows where that difference comes from.

On the six scenarios the network was trained on, bounding its influence changes almost nothing

it from becoming harmful where its training no longer applies.

\section{Generalisation}
\label{sec:generalisation}

Every policy scores lower on the unseen scenarios, which is expected: they were written to be
harder. What differs is how much each loses and what it sacrifices. The clearest case is
\texttt{heavy\_load}, a factory producing beyond what one robot can carry: the unbounded model
scores \num{33.78} against the bounded model's \num{54.88} ($p<0.001$), by far the largest effect of
the bound anywhere in the evaluation and the observation that motivated it. Both variants are safe;
the difference is entirely lost production, \num{30.06} against \num{21.84} units.

Two policies score higher there --- \texttt{ns\_neural\_only} with \num{77.73} and \texttt{ppo} with
\num{59.47} --- by working the battery down to \SI{3.4}{\percent} and \SI{1.4}{\percent} and
violating the reserve in \num{30} and \num{29} of their \num{30} shifts. Under
\cref{sec:constraints} these are not better solutions but solutions to a different, unconstrained
problem.

\section{Safety}
\label{sec:safety-results}

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.82\textwidth]{fig_violations.png}
    \caption{Share of shifts containing at least one safety violation (battery below the reserve
             away from the charger, motor temperature at the limit, or an empty battery).}
    \label{fig:violations}
\end{figure}

The result is categorical rather than gradual. Every policy that carries the symbolic hard rules
--- the \NS, its bounded and unbounded variants, the estimate-only and symbolic-only ablations and
the rule-based reference --- completed all \num{1800} of their shifts without a single violation.
Every policy without them violated constraints: the same network without rules in
\SI{15.8}{\percent} of shifts, masked \texttt{ppo} in \SI{14.7}{\percent}, unmasked \texttt{ppo} in
\SI{11.7}{\percent}.

For \texttt{ppo} the violations are concentrated where its training no longer covers the
situation: \num{49} of its \num{53} unsafe shifts occur in unseen scenarios, and \num{29} of those
in \texttt{heavy\_load} alone. Retraining PPO on all six training scenarios instead of four did not
change this (\texttt{ppo\_wp6}, \num{51}/\num{360}), which indicates that the violations follow from
learning the constraint from a reward signal rather than from having seen too few scenarios.

\section{Throughput and energy}
\label{sec:energy-results}

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.78\textwidth]{fig_energy_throughput.png}
    \caption{Delivered units against energy per delivered unit, averaged over all scenarios and
             seeds. Points towards the lower right deliver more for less energy.}
    \label{fig:energy-throughput}
\end{figure}

The rule-based reference is by far the most energy-efficient policy (\SI{0.099}{\watt\hour} per
delivered unit against \SI{0.150}{\watt\hour} for the \NS), achieved by charging often ---
\num{7.1} trips per shift against \num{2.8} --- and therefore spending much of the shift parked,
where idling is paid by the charger rather than the battery; its throughput suffers accordingly
(\num{51.6} units against \num{59.9}, \num{7.47} lost against \num{1.90}). That accounting
asymmetry is discussed in \cref{sec:limitations}. \texttt{ppo} sits at the opposite end: most units
delivered (\num{61.9}), most energy spent (\SI{10.21}{\watt\hour}) and the lowest mean minimum
battery of the safe-scoring policies (\SI{45.0}{\percent} against \SI{59.0}{\percent}).

\section{Decision latency}
\label{sec:latency}

Mean time per decision was \SI{1.46}{\milli\second} for the \NS, \SI{1.39}{\milli\second} for the
symbolic layer alone and \SI{2.67}{\milli\second} for \texttt{ppo}. Since one action lasts from
\SI{30}{\second} to several minutes, decision time is irrelevant to the result for every policy
tested; it is reported only to show that neither approach is disqualified by its computational
cost.

\section{Validation against the full simulation}
\label{sec:gazebo-validation}

All numbers so far come from the fast simulator. Twelve complete shifts were therefore driven in
Gazebo with the full navigation stack and each replayed in the fast simulator on the same policy,
scenario and seed (\cref{sec:gazebo-setup}).

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.6\textwidth]{fig_gazebo_vs_sim.png}
    \caption{Gazebo score against fast-simulator score for the same policy, scenario and seed. A
             point on the dashed line would mean perfect agreement.}
    \label{fig:gazebo-vs-sim}
\end{figure}

Over the eleven shifts that ran to completion the two agree closely on energy and slightly less on
throughput: \SI{3.24}{\watt\hour} against \SI{3.24}{\watt\hour} (\SI{+0.2}{\percent}), \num{23.7}
delivered units against \num{25.0} (\SI{-5.1}{\percent}), score \num{21.99} against \num{23.32}
(\SI{-5.7}{\percent}). Gazebo is consistently the weaker of the two: real driving loses a few
seconds per leg to acceleration, recovery behaviours and arrival tolerances, about one delivered
unit over a twenty-minute shift. No completed shift contained a violation on either side, and the
ordering of the policies was identical.

policies differ most; one sixty-minute shift was therefore driven per model on
\texttt{low\_battery\_start} (\cref{tab:longshift}).

With almost identical throughput (\num{61} against \num{62} units), \texttt{ppo} drove the battery

below \SI{17.0}{\percent}. The safety difference measured statistically in
\cref{sec:safety-results} therefore reproduces in the full simulation, with real navigation,
real timing and real recovery behaviour.

\section{Global path planner}
\label{sec:planner-study}

The decision layer chooses destinations; a global planner chooses the route. Five Nav2 planners
were compared with the same controller throughout, so that only the planner differs. On a short
benchmark --- a fixed seven-leg tour, five repeats each --- Theta$^{*}$ was fastest at
\SI{147.8(9)}{\second} against \SI{152.7(19)}{\second} for NavFn Dijkstra ($p=0.001$), with about
half the turning and the shortest planning times, and was made the default on that evidence.

Over full twenty-minute shifts the picture reversed. In five repetitions of an identical shift per
planner, three of the five Theta$^{*}$ shifts contained navigation faults --- six planner-loop
stalls, three refusals to plan and six navigation retries --- while all five NavFn shifts were
clean. The refusals carry their own explanation in the planner's message: \texttt{Either of the
start or goal pose are an obstacle!}. Theta$^{*}$ refuses to plan when the start or goal cell is
momentarily occupied in the costmap, which happens when the robot stands close to a machine;
NavFn plans from the nearest free cell within its tolerance of \SI{0.5}{\metre} instead. Counting
every Gazebo shift driven in this work, Theta$^{*}$ produced faults in \num{6} of \num{17} shifts
and NavFn in \num{0} of \num{5}. The speed advantage did not survive either: over whole shifts the
two planners scored \num{18.07} (sd \num{1.61}) and \num{17.75} (sd \num{1.23}), a difference well
inside the run-to-run spread. NavFn Dijkstra was therefore restored as the default.
